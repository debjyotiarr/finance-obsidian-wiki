# Wasserstein Penalisation as an Endogenous Breadth Constraint in Penalised Mean-Variance Portfolio Selection

## Abstract (verbatim from arXiv)
A manager who wants a long-only book and has read that Wasserstein-robust mean-variance selection improves risk-adjusted performance faces two decisions: how much robustness to buy, and how diversified to be. In the long-only problem, and for the penalised scalarisation of the robust objective, these are the same decision: the robustification term depends on the weight vector alone, so on the simplex the radius prices effective breadth and nothing else. The equivalence is specific to that scalarisation; it fails for the return-floored form, where the radius also raises the return threshold, and for the exact robust risk-minimisation problem. Two consequences follow. First, a negative one needing no alpha: positive definiteness of the covariance does not imply breadth, since no bound on the dispersion of the mean and no positive spectral floor prevents the long-only optimum from collapsing onto a single asset, so the breadth of the minimum-variance benchmark is an irreducible input. Second, identification: matched to a benchmark with the same effective number of holdings, the robust portfolio attains the same mean-variance value to solver precision, so the radius carries no in-sample information beyond breadth selection, and the residual weight difference lives in near-null directions of the covariance. On fourteen A-share panels the ex post comparison is exact by construction, hence no evidence. The informative test fixes breadth before the window is used: a cap set to the historical level of the robust portfolio's realised breadth recovers about five sixths of its gain, leaving a residual of seven to eleven basis points per day across five tracking rules, whereas a fixed target recovers only a quarter to two fifths. The robust portfolio is thus a breadth rule, not a fixed breadth choice. We close with a reporting standard that makes the attribution checkable.
